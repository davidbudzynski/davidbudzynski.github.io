In a corporate world every meeting now comes with an AI aide that happily spits
out four pages of transcript-flavoured "notes" nobody will ever read. I go the
other way: a fountain pen, decent ink, good paper, and two thirds of a page
that is actually useful.

This comes from a long-time Org mode user, not a pen-and-paper romantic. For
many years I kept my work notes in Org mode and I still think it is great. But
after moving jobs I ran into the usual corporate friction: different
permissions, different accesses, and Emacs on Windows do not go well together.
Paper has no IT policy, needs no sync approval, and survives a job change
untouched. So for work notes, I turned to pen and paper — and stayed because
it turned out to be better at the job, not just easier to set up. More on the
Org mode side of this in my [C++ notebooks in Org mode post][3].

## You cannot write as fast as they talk

That is the whole point. Typing — or auto-transcription — keeps up with speech,
so it does not have to choose. Handwriting cannot, so it must. You listen,
decide what matters, and distill it into a sentence or two.

A good meeting note is not a record. It is a decision about what was
important: what was decided, who owns what, what the open question is. The
slowness of the pen forces that decision in the moment, while the AI summary
defers it forever — and deferred usually means never.

## Already digested, no re-read needed

Meta-analyses on handwriting versus typing lean slightly toward handwriting
[Flanigan et al.][1], but that is not why I do it. For me it is simpler: I am
already digesting the info during the meeting, so I do not need to re-read it
after. My two thirds of a page *are* the understanding, done once, in the
moment.

The other half is trust. Every todo, name, or follow-up is written down, so it
stops buzzing around my head and overloading my brain. If I wrote it down, I
trust I will return to it and make sure it gets taken care of. The notebook is
my inbox — nothing lives in my head rent-free.

## Thinking and planning on paper

I reach for pen and paper in two situations: thinking through something, and
meetings. Both are the same activity, really — turning a mess of input into a
small number of lines that matter.

For thinking and planning it is sketches, arrows, crossed-out dead ends. Paper
takes any shape without asking me to pick a template or a palette first. No
battery, no notifications, no tab begging for attention. The friction is so low
it disappears, which is exactly what you want when the thought itself is
half-formed.

For meetings it is dates, names, decisions, and a small box around the action
items. If it does not fit on one side of a page, I probably did not understand
it yet.

## Good tools matter

This will sound familiar if you read my [split keyboard post][2]: once you do
something every day, the tool stops being incidental. A scratchy ballpoint on
copy paper makes you want to stop writing. A pen you like, ink that shades a
little, paper that does not feather — that makes you want to keep going.

My current setup (still evolving):

- Pens: [LAMY Safari][4], [Pilot Custom 74][5], [Platinum 3776 Century][6].
  The Safari is the workhorse — cheap enough to carry everywhere, tough enough
  to survive a bag. The Custom 74 and the 3776 are the step up for longer
  writing.
- Inks: the classic is [Waterman Serenity Blue][7]. I have been using it since
  I was a primary school kid, and I still have notes from 15 years ago written
  in it that look like the day I wrote them. For more adventurous days:
  [Sailor Souten][8], [Waterman Inspired Blue][7], [Waterman Tender Purple][7].
  For black that has to survive, [Sailor Kiwaguro][9] — pigmented, water and
  UV resistant, if that is a concern.
- Paper: [Oxford Campus][10] refill pads, kept in binders — something I really
  loved upon moving to the UK. For quick notes and carrying into meetings, a
  reporter notepad. And [Rhodia][11] notebooks, which I think are genuinely
  excellent quality: smooth, fountain-pen friendly, no feathering.

Nothing exotic. The rule is the same as with keyboards or dotfiles: pick a few
things, learn them properly, and let the habit compound.

## Against the four-page summary

I am not anti-AI-notes. A transcript is a fine safety net when you need to
check exactly what was said. But a safety net is not a summary, and a summary
is not understanding.

The AI gives you everything, which is another way of giving you nothing to
hold on to. The pen gives you two thirds of a page you actually thought about
— and that, in my experience, is what gets read, remembered, and acted on.
